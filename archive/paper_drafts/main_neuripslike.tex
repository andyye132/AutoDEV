\title{Do Discovered Game-Testing Programs Generalize?\\Autonomous Discovery of Exploration Strategies That Find Real Bugs in Unseen Games}
\author{Andy Ye \\ University of Washington}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Game studios use reinforcement-learning agents to test games, but the exploration strategies that drive those testers are designed by
hand, and nobody knows whether a strategy that finds one kind of bug finds others. We ask whether an autonomous research loop can
discover a better one. We take Kinetix, a suite of 2D physics games, deliberately break levels in five ways (missing boundaries,
non-solid obstacles, unintended goals, runaway physics, disarmed hazards) with a witness state for each, and let an LLM-driven
evolutionary loop write tester \emph{programs} --- full exploration procedures over save/restore-able simulator states --- keeping
those that reach witness states fastest. The decisive test is transfer: 30\% of levels and one entire defect class (leaving the arena)
were hidden from the search. \textbf{Qualifying result:} the discovered tester scores 0.666 on the search split vs.\ 0.566 for the strongest
hand-designed tester (action-repeat random), 0.617 vs.\ 0.575 on held-out levels, and 0.418 vs.\ 0.266 on the held-out class (47\% vs.\ 30\% of escape defects witnessed; 5 seeds, 95\% CIs $\le$0.015); run on
74 shipped and 64 freshly generated levels with generic engine oracles, it finds [N] replay-verified real anomalies (severity A/B)
against [M] for the best hand-designed tester, including a block launched at 16\,m/s that passes through a boundary wall's face and buries itself 10\,cm in the floor before the solver ejects it.
The tester, the transfer analysis and Part~1 were produced by the autonomous loop; the authors built and verified the benchmark and
document the process, every human intervention, and the recomputation of every number.
\end{abstract}
