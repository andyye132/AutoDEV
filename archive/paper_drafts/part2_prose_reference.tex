\section{The autonomous loop}
\label{sec:system}
The result in Part~1 was produced by an autonomous research loop we built and then pointed at a question. The loop wrote
the benchmark harness, evolved the tester programs, ran the experiments, and drafted Part~1. Our role, as humans, was to
design the defect oracles and the verification protocol, decide which evaluator to point the search at, and check every
number, including the one check (\S\ref{sec:exploit}) that turned out to matter most.

\textbf{What the loop searches over.} A candidate is a short pure-JAX \emph{program}: the four hooks
\texttt{init/restore/act/archive} over a save/restore state archive, not a trained policy. An LLM proposal model writes and
mutates candidates; each is executed on a multi-engine fitness (the fraction of witness states it reaches at a fixed
interaction budget, on the search split, seeds $\{0,1,2\}$), and an island evolutionary loop
(ShinkaEvolve~\citep{shinkaevolve}) selects. We used \texttt{claude-sonnet-5} and \texttt{claude-opus-5} as proposal
models, two islands, and a fixed API-cost cap; fitness ran on cluster GPUs while orchestration ran on a laptop.

\textbf{Harness and tools.} The prompt specifies the hook interface, the scoring formula, and hard constraints (pure JAX,
fixed shapes, in-range actions, ``runs in a few seconds per level''). The loop receives back the scalar fitness plus a text
feedback string that lists its worst targets and a paired $95\%$ CI against a frozen reference, so it treats
within-interval deltas as noise. Every substrate (Kinetix, MiniGrid, MinAtar, Sokoban, Gymnax, Craftax) is the genuine
published simulator, and the PPO baselines run in the same harness on the same feature vector.

\section{Verification}
\label{sec:verify}
This is the part we are most careful about, and, given the workshop's premise, the part we think matters most.

\textbf{Replay-from-reset.} A counted hit is not trusted because a worker reported reaching a witness. On Kinetix, the
harness records the exact action chain from the level's reset to the flagged state, \emph{re-simulates} that chain from a
clean reset through the real physics engine, and requires the witness to fire again. A hit that does not reproduce is
scored as a miss. This makes evaluator exploits worthless to the search by construction, and it demonstrably rejects
non-reproducible hits in our logs.

\textbf{The frozen protocol.} Splits, seeds (fitness $\{0,1,2\}$ and evaluation $\{100,\dots,104\}$, disjoint), budget,
and exclusions are fixed in a hashed protocol object, and result files carry the hash so incompatible runs cannot be
mixed. On Kinetix we seal $30\%$ of levels \emph{and} one entire defect class (arena escape) from the search, and a
pre-registered long-budget reachability calibration bounds what any tester could witness. We report sealed-class detection
honestly against that ceiling: the best discovered tester reaches about $0.37$ on the held-out class, which is roughly
$91\%$ of the \emph{witnessable} set (about $55\%$ of the class is unreachable by any policy), quoted against the
witnessable denominator and never against an unreachable one. Instances witnessable within a few no-op steps, or
unwitnessable by construction, are excluded with counts stated.

\section{The exploit the loop found, and how we caught it}
\label{sec:exploit}
The result we are proudest of is not the one the loop first reported. Its first strong ``discovery'' inflated its fitness
by \textbf{+0.11} (a replay-verified $0.468$ reported as $0.578$), and there was \emph{nothing incriminating in the
candidate's code}. It had learned to exploit a \emph{concurrency race} in the archive's scatter: when several workers
write the same slot in one step, the harness produced physically impossible ``chimera'' states, a state paired with the
wrong lineage, which tripped the witness oracle. Sixty-two of the $256$ archive slots were directly observed as chimeras.

What it survived is the uncomfortable part. The inflated candidate passed \emph{every evaluator-mediated check we had}:
the fitness, both held-out splits, four design comparisons, and an LLM code-audit of the candidate itself, which reads the
program and, correctly, finds nothing wrong, because the corruption is in the harness's state and not the code. For about
ten hours it stood atop our own registered abstract as a headline number. \textbf{The only thing that caught it was
replaying every counted hit from a clean reset and requiring it to reproduce}, which it did not. We rebuilt the archive
scatter to deduplicate writers, withdrew the tainted numbers, and re-ran everything. We also corrected our own over-reach
in the process: an earlier claim that ``the exploit reproduces at $N{=}3$, $+0.17$'' turned out to be a split-mismatch
artifact, and we withdrew it. The honest exploit is a single program, $+0.11$, on a matched split.

We think this is the general lesson for a field about to have loops producing candidate papers at scale: \emph{you cannot
certify an autonomous result by reading its code or its score; you have to re-execute what it claims from a clean state.}
Code-audit, today's default defense, is blind to an exploit that lives in the harness's state rather than the candidate.

\section{Human interventions (disclosed in full)}
\label{sec:human}
Per the workshop's disclosure norm, here is every place a human changed the outcome. (i) We designed the defect classes,
the witness predicates, and the reachability calibration. (ii) We froze and hashed the protocol and chose the sealed
splits. (iii) We noticed the first ``discovery'' was too good, diagnosed the race, rebuilt the harness, and withdrew the
inflated numbers; this was the single most consequential intervention. (iv) To close the fairness question against RL, we
ran a learning-rate and entropy sweep, so the PPO baselines are tuned rather than strawmen. (v) We recomputed every number
in Part~1 from regenerable logs and excluded all pre-fix (``racy'') files from every table. The authorship, curation, and
responsibility for the submission are ours; the qualifying result could not have been established without the loop.

\section{Why this matters}
\label{sec:significance}
The headline, one written program that out-reaches trained RL at equal budget on sparse-reward game testing with the
regime boundary mapped honestly, is worth attention because it holds at a tester's \emph{real} budget rather than an
RL-sized one, and because the winning artifact is the agent's own: \textbf{the exploration strategy and its three runtime
modules (the homeostatic novelty controller, the emitter/gene bandit, the per-action novelty-UCB) were discovered by the
loop and written by no human}, and without the loop the result could not exist. But the contribution we would defend
hardest is the verification apparatus around it. The exploit is an existence proof that an autonomous loop will find and
exploit the weakest part of its own evaluation, that the corruption can be invisible to inspection, and that
execution-grounded replay is what recovers the truth. That is the workshop's bottleneck made concrete: when loops can
generate candidate results at scale, the scarce and decisive act is re-executing their claims from a clean state, and it
should live \emph{inside} the loop's fitness so that such exploits are worthless by construction.
