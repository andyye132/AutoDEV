Game and simulation testing needs exploration policies that drive a system into target or defect states; the standard approach trains a reinforcement-learning explorer per game. We show that an autonomous research loop can instead write such a tester as a short program, and that at an equal environment-interaction budget this zero-training program reaches target states faster than a trained PPO explorer across eight structurally different RL environments -- grid navigation, key-and-door subgoals, arcade, puzzle/planning, continuous control, deep-exploration, 2D physics, and crafting/survival. Against a tuned PPO it never loses -- a clear win on six and a tie on two -- and on the four sparse-reward navigation/planning tasks it is the single best method, beating random, sustained-action, and tuned RL alike. The advantage is not the save/restore archive it uses (a plain Go-Explore baseline with the same archive loses to both PPO and random on the hardest task); it is the specific strategy the loop discovers, and it is a genuine held-out-engine transfer: one program evolved on a multi-engine fitness beats task-specific PPO on engines it never saw. The advantage is strictly equal-budget: with tuning and more budget RL matches the tester on the reward-dense and trivially-reachable tasks, but at the budget a tester actually gets, the zero-training program wins the sparse-reward tasks outright. On a deep Kinetix substrate -- a replay-verified benchmark of ~790 planted physics defects with sealed splits and reachability calibration -- the same testers beat every hand-designed, one-shot-LLM, and RL tester at equal budget, and a difficulty-graded bug benchmark shows structured testers (including ours) find harder bugs than trained RL as difficulty scales. The benchmark, the testers, and this paper's primary section were produced by the loop; the authors built the oracles, verified every number, and document every intervention -- including a harness exploit the loop found and the replay check that caught it.
